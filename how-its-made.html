The big idea

What is 3D printing?

[PLACEHOLDER] A 3D printer builds an object by stacking up thin layers of material, one on top of another, until the whole shape exists.

Most things in your house were made by cutting material away or squirting it into a mold. 3D printing works the other way around: it starts with nothing and adds material only where it's needed.

The most common kind of home printer works a lot like a robot-controlled hot glue gun. It melts a plastic thread and draws each layer with it, thinner than a credit card, over and over, until your object is done.

From idea to object

The four steps

  1. Photo: 3D model on screen 4:3

    Find or design a model

    [PLACEHOLDER] Every print starts as a 3D model, a digital file that describes the shape. You can design your own with free, beginner-friendly tools like Tinkercad, or download one of millions of ready-made models from sites like Printables, MakerWorld or Thingiverse.

  2. Photo: slicer software 4:3

    Slice it

    [PLACEHOLDER] A program called a slicer cuts the model into hundreds of flat layers, like slicing a loaf of bread. It works out the exact path the printer should follow, how solid the inside should be, and where temporary supports are needed to hold up overhanging parts.

  3. Photo or video: printer mid-print 4:3

    Print it

    [PLACEHOLDER] The printer heats its nozzle to around 200°C, melts the plastic and draws the first layer onto the bed. Then it rises a fraction of a millimeter and draws the next one. A keychain might take 20 minutes; a big sculpture can take a day or more.

  4. Photo: finished, painted print 4:3

    Finish it

    [PLACEHOLDER] Snap off any supports, and the print is ready. Or keep going: sand it smooth, paint it, glue pieces together or add lights and magnets. This is where a print turns into a piece of art.

What it's made of

Materials

[PLACEHOLDER] Home printers mostly use plastic, but not all plastic is the same.

PLA

The everyday favorite. Made from plants like corn, easy to print, low smell and available in every color you can think of.

PETG

The tough one. The same family of plastic as water bottles, so it handles knocks, sunshine and a bit of heat.

TPU

The bendy one. Rubbery and flexible, great for phone cases, grips and squishy toys.

Resin

The detailed one. A liquid hardened by light, layer by layer, for super-fine details like miniatures and jewelry.

Cheat sheet

Words you'll hear

[PLACEHOLDER] Makers love their jargon. Here's what it all means.

Filament
The plastic "thread" a printer melts, sold on spools.
Nozzle
The hot metal tip the melted plastic comes out of.
Print bed
The flat surface the object is built on.
Layer height
How thick each layer is. Thinner layers look smoother but take longer.
Infill
The pattern inside a print. Most prints are partly hollow to save time and plastic.
Supports
Temporary scaffolding that holds up overhangs, snapped off afterward.
Slicer
Software that turns a 3D model into instructions for the printer.
STL / 3MF
The most common file types for 3D models.
Good questions

Questions people ask

Is it hard to get started?

[PLACEHOLDER] Not anymore. Many newer printers come mostly assembled and calibrate themselves, so you can go from unboxing to a first print in an afternoon.

Do I need my own printer?

[PLACEHOLDER] Nope. Many public libraries and makerspaces have printers you can use, and print services (like us) can print a model for you.

How long does a print take?

[PLACEHOLDER] Anywhere from a few minutes to a few days. It depends on the size, how detailed it is and how solid it needs to be.

Is it safe? Does it smell?

[PLACEHOLDER] PLA, the most common plastic, has very little smell. It's still a good idea to print in a room with some fresh air. Resin printing needs more care: gloves and good ventilation.

Can I print anything?

[PLACEHOLDER] Almost. Prints are limited by the size of the printer, and some shapes need supports. And just like photocopying a book, printing someone else's design is only okay if their license allows it.
